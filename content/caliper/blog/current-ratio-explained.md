Current ratio is current assets divided by current liabilities. If your current assets are ₹1.5 crore and current liabilities are ₹1 crore, the ratio is 1.5. For every rupee you owe in the next twelve months, you have ₹1.50 in assets that should turn into cash in the same period.

Banks in India look at this number when they set or renew working capital limits. Many still use 1.33 as a reference point, a habit that goes back to the Tandon Committee norms.

## What it hides

The ratio treats every rupee of current assets the same. Your business doesn't.

**Slow debtors.** ₹40 lakh owed by a customer who has not paid in 200 days counts the same as cash in the bank. The ratio looks fine. Your cash flow does not.

**Old stock.** Inventory that has not moved in a year is still a current asset on paper. You may never sell it at book value.

**Timing.** A ratio of 1.5 on 31 March says nothing about the week in June when GST, salaries and a supplier payment all fall due together.

## Reading it properly

Look at the current ratio next to two other numbers:

- **Quick ratio**, which leaves out inventory. If the current ratio is healthy but the quick ratio is low, your liquidity depends on selling stock.
- **Receivable days**, which tells you how long customers take to pay. A rising number means more of your current assets are stuck with customers.
